The Trillion-Dollar Case for Biochar

Everyone is talking about AI as the foundation of the next industrial revolution. While they're definitely correct, I'm here to assert that there is another revolution taking shape beneath our feet.

Biochar is the foundation of an emerging trillion-dollar industrial category. It is not a single product serving a single market, biochar is a platform for transforming one of the world’s most abundant and underutilized resources, low-value biomass, into engineered biomaterials that serve essential functions across the global economy.

Biochar creates value through three fundamental mechanisms:

  1. It replaces existing inputs, such as peat, filtration media, and fossil carbon.
  2. It improves the performance of existing systems, such as agricultural soils, industrial filtration, and construction materials.
  3. It transforms waste into valuable outputs, including engineered products, useful energy, and durable carbon removal.

But "raw" biochar straight from the pyrolysis reactor is rarely good enough to plug into existing systems. Biochar's properties vary significantly depending on the feedstock and production conditions, making it unsuitable for many industrial applications without further processing.

The solution is to engineer raw biochar through methods such as activation, particle sizing, blending, and other forms of post-processing. These techniques transform a generic carbon material into specialized products tailored to the technical requirements of different industries. Engineered biochar expands the range of viable applications, improves performance, and enables higher-value products.

This is how foundational industrial categories are built: not around a single application, but around a versatile material that becomes useful across many sectors. Cement, steel, and ammonia became pillars of the global economy because they evolved into standardized platforms upon which entire industries were built. Biochar has the same potential.

The opportunity is therefore not limited to creating a new carbon-removal market. It is to build a new industrial layer for the global economy, one that transforms waste into infrastructure, replaces extractive materials, improves the systems upon which modern life depends, and permanently removes carbon in the process.
